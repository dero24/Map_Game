# New England

**Covers:** Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut. Three
sub-zones: the *northern forest* (northern and western Maine, the White Mountains and north
of them, Vermont's Green Mountains and Northeast Kingdom), the *central hardwoods* (southern
NH and VT, interior MA, CT, RI), and the *coast* (Maine's rocky spruce coast; Cape Cod, the
Islands and the RI/CT shore, sandy with pitch pine and salt marsh).

**Feel:** stone walls running through second-growth woods, white clapboard villages around a
green, sugar maples, birch and hemlock, granite ledges and boulders, ponds and bogs; bright fall,
long snowy winter, a cold rocky sea.

**Street and yard trees:** Norway maple (INV, the most common street tree of older towns),
red maple, sugar maple (old village roads; suffering from road salt), honeylocust, littleleaf
linden, pin oak, London plane (Boston, Providence, Hartford), Callery pear (INV, spreading in
southern NE), Kwanzan cherry, crabapple, flowering and kousa dogwood (south of central MA),
Japanese maple, paper birch clumps, Colorado blue spruce, arborvitae hedges, white pine screens,
survivor American elms on town greens (New Haven's "Elm City" namesakes), old apple trees in
dooryards, lilac by farmhouse doors (NH state flower), forsythia, rhododendron, mountain laurel
(CT state flower) in yards. Shore towns: Japanese black pine, Rosa rugosa hedges, hydrangeas.

**Epiphytes, vines and hangers**

- Old man's beard and other Usnea lichens: pale gray-green beards hanging from spruce and fir on the foggy Maine coast and in the mountains.
- Leafy lichens and moss on north sides of trunks and on old stone walls.
- Poison ivy (hairy vines up trunks, groundcover on dunes), Virginia creeper (crimson in fall),
  wild grape, oriental bittersweet (INV, smothering roadside trees with orange-red berries),
  Boston ivy on brick (college towns).
- No Spanish moss, no kudzu (a few tiny outbreaks have been reported in CT/MA but treat as absent).

**Seasonal looks**

- *Fall:* peak color late September (north, mountains) to late October (coast and CT);
  red maples scarlet, sugar maples orange, birch and beech yellow, oaks bronze last. Lowbush
  blueberry barrens go crimson. Bare by mid-November except oaks and young beech.
- *Winter (Dec-Mar):* snow cover; dark conifers, white birch, tan beech leaves rattling;
  frozen ponds with ice fishing shanties; salt-white roads.
- *Spring:* red maple flowers a red haze in late March; "mud season" brown in April;
  shadbush white late April; forsythia, magnolia, apple and lilac in May; black-fly season in
  the north woods mid-May to June.
- *Summer:* deep green, lupines in June, hay fields, fog on the Maine coast, low tide rockweed.

### Regional signature

1. Sugar and red maples in October (with white church, stone wall and paper birch).
2. Eastern white pine and hemlock over granite boulders, ferns and mossy stone walls.
3. Moose in a northern bog at dawn.
4. Common loon on a lake (and its call at night).
5. Black-capped chickadee at the feeder in snow; wild turkey flocks in yards.
6. Maine's rocky coast: spruce and fir with Usnea beards, rockweed, harbor seals, lobster buoys, common eiders.
7. Lowbush blueberry barrens red in fall; Rosa rugosa on every beach path.
8. Cape Cod: pitch pine, scrub oak, salt marsh and gray seals.
9. Red efts on wet trails.
10. Beech leaves rattling tan through the winter woods.

### Range notes

- Moose fade south of central MA; deer fade in the deep north woods in winter.
- No venomous snakes in Maine. Timber rattlesnake and copperhead are tiny relict populations: effectively never seen.
- Opossum, Carolina wren, tufted titmouse, red-bellied woodpecker, northern mockingbird and black vulture are all recent arrivals that thin out going north.
- Fiddler crabs and horseshoe crabs mostly south of Cape Cod and in Cape Cod Bay.
- No Spanish moss, kudzu, armadillos, fire ants or anoles.
